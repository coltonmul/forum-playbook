6 Past Presidents and a 20-Year Executive Director Weigh In on the Most Important Things to Remember When Building a Board

Compiled by Colton Mulligan, incoming president of EO Nashville (2027-28). Shared by Colton, not published by EO.

Real quick: Colton is EO Nashville's incoming president, and he wanted to be very intentional about his board. So he sent ten of the chapter's past presidents six questions. Six answered: five in writing and one on a recorded phone call. He also worked through the whole slate, seat by seat, with Sameera Lowe, the chapter's executive director of 20 years.

This is the long version, with their actual words. If you are a president-elect anywhere, it is yours to use.

What Colton asked

The six questions

  1. AWhat are the 3-5 most important board seats? (I'm anticipating that some board members flake throughout the year, and I want to make sure that I focus on commitments from the most important roles.)
  2. BWas there anything intentional you did during the "board building" season of approaching people to garner commitments, or set expectations?
  3. CHow much guidance, direction or vision did you cast during your board building, or was it just as simple as making asks to people who you thought would do a good job?
  4. DWhat are the "soft" board seats, a.k.a. the seats where it's okay if they flake or fail, because the roles aren't as impactful?
  5. EAny red flags or advice you'd have for what to consider as we start the Jenga game of board selection and strategizing?
  6. FWhat was your greatest regret in setting your board, and what are you thankful that you did, when setting your board?
If you read nothing else

Seven things to remember

  • Lock in Learning and Membership first. Every president who ranked seats named Learning, and four of five named Membership. Finance came next. These are the asks where you say out loud: please do not flake on me.
  • Pick people, not a manifesto. Nobody cast a big vision. They asked people they trusted to do a good job, aimed at two or three priorities, because a year goes very fast.
  • Your executive director is half the work. One president put it at fifty percent. Lean on their read of who is ready, and decide together who makes each ask before anyone asks anyone.
  • Look past the usual hands. Add a few people who have never been involved, would be good at it, and would get something out of it. A seat is a give and a get.
  • Soft seats exist, but watch two of them. Governance "is not important, until it is," and one president called Member Engagement the most underused seat in any chapter, because renewals are the scoreboard.
  • Two years per seat, and a yes is not a commitment. People get tired, and anyone who checks every committee box tends to commit to none of them.
  • The only regret worth fearing is putting someone on your board who is not all in. None of the four who answered that question named a regret of their own.
AThe 3-5 most important seats

The seats that cannot flake

What are the 3-5 most important board seats?

Learning was unanimous. Membership was nearly unanimous, and the one holdout picked Member Engagement, which is membership by another name. The logic was the same every time: these seats touch the member experience, and the member experience is what members pay for. Finance came third, less for the workload than for the risk.

BBoard-building season

What they did on purpose

Was there anything intentional you did during the "board building" season to garner commitments or set expectations?

Mostly nothing fancy. One answered with a single word: "Nope." The moves worth stealing: trust your executive director's read on who is ready to move up, recruit beyond the people already raising their hands, keep a great chair for a second year, and use the perks of the seat. A covered trip to EO's Global Leadership Conference tips people toward yes.

CVision, or just asking?

Pick people, then pick two things

How much guidance, direction or vision did you cast, or was it as simple as making asks to people you thought would do a good job?

Every answer leaned toward the ask. Nobody described a vision deck or a kickoff speech. They thought about who would do a good job and who would get something out of the seat. The one piece of vision that came up: choose a couple of things you want to get done, and staff those.

DThe soft seats

Where a flake costs the least

What are the "soft" seats, where it's okay if they flake or fail?

Engagement, Mentorship, Special Projects, Community, and "most of the last three rows" of the roster. Two cautions came with it. A seat nobody can define (Community, for one chapter) should get a real job or come off the board. And a big board only works when strong staff carry the load underneath it.

ERed flags and advice

Before you start the Jenga game

Any red flags or advice for the Jenga game of board selection?

Skip the slackers you already know about, and the people who do not play nice. Then run the foxhole test: who do you want next to you if something goes sideways in public? Call every person rolling off the board yourself. Start renewals early. And know that it takes longer than it looks.

FRegrets and gratitude

The only regret worth fearing

What was your greatest regret in setting your board, and what are you thankful you did?

Four presidents answered this one, and none named a regret of their own. One named the regret worth fearing anyway: a board member who is not all in. What they were thankful for was the people themselves, which is the strongest argument for choosing people you would enjoy spending a year with.

Read this before you treat any answer as gospel

Where they disagreed

Member Engagement

Two presidents called it a soft seat. A third called it the most underused seat in any chapter, because it is the direct line to renewals. Both can be true: it is soft when it only runs new-member meetings, and it is critical when someone owns renewals.

Governance

Two put it in their top five. One called it soft, then added the line everyone remembers: "not important, until it is." Staff it with someone steady even if it is quiet most months.

Forum

Two ranked it high. One of them added that it is probably less important now that the chapter has full-time forum staff, and another president never worried about the forum seat because it was already covered. With strong forum staff, a weak forum chair costs less than it used to. That will vary by chapter.

One idea that came out of the calls

A co-chair whose whole job is renewals

On the recorded call, Colton floated a Member Engagement co-chair with one target: renewals. Their season runs roughly January through May. They work across Learning, Social and Forum, use the staff's list of members who are flight risks, and answer one question every month: what are we doing this month to keep the most members? The past president on the call liked it. If renewals are the scoreboard, somebody on the board should own the scoreboard.

"May you break something, resolve something, and champion something that makes the chapter just a bit better!!"
